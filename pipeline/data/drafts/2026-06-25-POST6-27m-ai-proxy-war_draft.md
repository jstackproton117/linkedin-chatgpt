AI companies just spent $27 million on a single congressional primary. The candidate they backed lost.

In June 2026, in New York's 12th district, Alex Bores — a former tech worker running for Congress — lost a primary race in which AI-aligned PACs and corporate donors spent heavily on his behalf. The Verge called it "the $27 million AI proxy war."

The common read: AI companies are flexing political muscle. One race, notable story, move on.

The more uncomfortable read: the companies building these tools are now actively buying political outcomes. Not lobbying for abstract policy positions. Attempting to elect specific people who will vote specific ways on AI regulation.

For engineering leaders, this matters in a way that goes beyond who wins a primary.

The tools we build, the platforms we ship, the systems we design — they're no longer just products. They're infrastructure for an industry that is now explicitly trying to shape its own regulatory environment.

I'm not saying that's right or wrong. I'm saying we should be clear-eyed about what we're part of.

The engineering organizations I respect most are careful about the distinction between what their technology can do and what it should do. That principle doesn't stop at the product boundary. It extends to the industry context we operate in.

$27 million on one congressional primary is a data point. Watch the trend it represents.
